Sounth Africa's Energy Situation

Energy systems are layered. Progress in one layer can hide vulnerability in another. For south africa:

  1. Coal phase out
  2. Expansion of renewables
  3. Role reversals in regional trade

These interactions must be managed carefully.

South Africa is a useful example because its electricity system has undergone significant stress and transformation in recent years. Being the most industrialized economy in Africa, reliability is required to sustain economic acitivity.

Operational Improvement

Starting in 2007, significant energy shortages led to an electricity crisis with 205 days of load shedding in 2022. This has commonly been attributed to insuffcient maintenance of coal plants and poor governance. Significant progress has since been made with no load shedding since March 2024. While plans to phase out coal by 2040 are in place, Eskom's operational recovery has been driven by reparations to some coal plants, restoring capacity to the grid. This saw energy availability (EAF) reaching or exceeding the 70% milestone on 83 occasions in 2025-26. OCGT usage also continues to fall, reducing the pressure on fuel price for other uses such as transportation.

OCGT have low thermal efficiency ranging 34-36% (eskom source) and are expensive, meant to run as peaker plants for events of very high demand or supply shortages. Not meant to run to meet ordinary demand. The decline in OCGT usage provides signifcant cost savings.

Coal phase-out vs renewables deployment

Aside the coal plants for which decomisdioning is delayed, old rundown coal plants are being repurposed for RE. Policy changes have also allowed businesses and households to employ private embedded generation, reducing pressure on the grid. This includes solar and batteties. Coal retirement schedules are outpacing the added renewable capacity. Repurposing of the coal plants requires significant construction so capacity overall for the nation drops.

Regional Electricity Trade

South Africa has been a net exporter in the SAPP for the majority of its years in operation since 1995. In recent years, the net value has gotten closer to zero.

The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) supports renewable planning and regional coordination. IRENA organizes initiatives such as the African Clean Energy Corridor (ACEC), which includes 21 countries across Eastern and Southern Africa. This initiative aims to strengthen cooperation between the Southern African Power Pool and the Eastern African Power Pool and promote the development of a North–South power transmission corridor.

From IRENA's report [1], in all scenarios, South Africa becomes the single largest net importer of electricity in the entire region by 2040, at 62 TWh net imports in the reference scenario. This is a complete structural reversal from SA's role as a net exporter in the SAPP.

Significant exporters by 2040 would be the DRC (51 TWh net exports) and Mozambique (31 TWh net exports). The Mozambique–South Africa link is specifically flagged as a high-priority transmission project. SA's imports would represent roughly 16% of its domestic demand.

The report projects SA's generation mix shifting significantly toward solar PV and wind by 2040. Solar and wind generation in SA alone would be 65–80 GW. But the key tension is that SA's coal retirement happens faster than its renewable buildout can compensate, creating a growing import dependence.

What Comes Next?

Cross-border trade is essential for cost minimisation. Building more transmission capacity pays through efficiency gains — savings exceed the infrastructure investment costs. Countries with surplus renewable generation (mainly DRC hydro, Mozambique hydro, and areas with strong wind and solar) can export to deficit areas, reducing the need for every country to build its own backup capacity. Without expanded trade, countries would need to build more expensive peaking plants or curtail cheap renewable energy.

The report shows that even in scenarios with very high VRE penetration, trade remains valuable because solar and wind profiles in different countries are complementary — when SA's solar is low, DRC's hydro is available.

Private solar reduces SA's immediate demand on Eskom, which helps in the short term. But the 2021 IRENA report shows the long-term trajectory is SA becoming more dependent on regional imports — regardless of what happens with private solar. Is the region prepared to meet the demand that SA will create?

Private embedded generation also means wealthier households/businesses can exit the shared system — equity angle.

IRENA Scenario Cases

Reference (REF) — The baseline. Cost-optimized, no renewable targets or CO2 limits, interconnectors limited to identified projects. Results in 36% solar/wind by 2040.

VRELim — Caps solar and wind at 20% by 2040 (5% by 2020, 10% by 2030), simulating slow or blocked deployment due to non-cost barriers.

VREHigh — Pushes solar and wind to 50% by 2040, testing what additional investment an ambitious renewables target would require.

HyDry (Dry-year) — Reduces hydropower capacity factors to simulate drought conditions, reflecting rising drought frequency in the region.

HyDel (Delayed hydro) — Delays hydropower project construction: 5 years for plants over 250 MW, 10 years for those over 1,000 MW.

TxNoLim — Removes build limits on key cross-border interconnectors after 2030 (e.g., Ethiopia–Kenya, Tanzania–Malawi) to assess how greater regional grid integration affects the power mix and trade.
