Recovery and Constraint in South Africa's Power System

This analysis discusses South Africa's recovery from an electricity crisis, the resulting constraints, and the role of regional expansion in addressing them.

Eskom • 2025-26
Eskom Recovery Coal Transition Renewable Integration Africa Clean Energy Corridor

System Overview

Beginning in 2007, South Africa experienced growing energy shortages that developed into a major electricity crisis, which peaked in 2022 with 205 days of load shedding. This crisis has commonly been attributed to insufficient maintenance of coal-fired power stations and challenges with governance. However, significant progress has since been made with no load shedding since March 2024. There are several layers that play a role in South Africa's changing energy landscape:

  1. Operational recovery: reparation to coal plants solve the reliability problem
  2. Coal repurposing and renewable deployment: introduces flexibility and capacity issues
  3. Regional trade: resolves this by sharing capacity and flexibility across borders

With constantly shifting factors (i.e. future demand, weather patterns, fuel prices, technology costs, political decisions), each solution introduces new constraints, requiring constant recalibration and careful planning.

Operational Recovery

Reduced load shedding in South Africa is primarily driven by improved performance of coal power stations, as well as the adoption of private renewable energy.[2] Over the financial year 2025-26, Eskom's Energy Availability Factor (EAF) reached or exceeded the 70% milestone on 83 occasions. More than 4100 MW of private capacity, mostly embedded solar, was added in the first half of 2025.[3]

Diesel-fired Open Cycle Gas Turbines (OCGTs) were previously used to reduce the severity of load-shedding, and have seen a significant decline in usage over 2025. These units are both inefficient (34-36% thermal efficiency) and costly, with diesel generation approximately 11 times more expensive than coal in 2025 (6084R/MWh vs 546R/MWh).[4]

Between April 2025 and March 2026, OCGT generation reduced by 460 GWh, corresponding to an approximate reduction in diesel expenditure of R1.87bn.

Coal Repurposing & Renewables Deployment

While reliability of power supply has improved, South Africa's long-term energy strategy introduces a different set of constraints: flexibility and supply gaps.

South Africa's coal fleet was designed for steady baseload operation, where output remains above a minimum stable load (MSL). Operating below this threshold can create uneven thermal gradients across boilers, leading to thermal stress and long-term material fatigue. This lack of flexibility contributes to grid congestion as renewable capacity is added, increasing the need for curtailment.[6]

Alongside this, long-term plans to reduce coal capacity create the challenge to replace the lost baseload power on schedule. Eskom plans to decommission nine coal power plants by 2035 [5] and repurpose them for renewable energy, with a goal of increasing capacity to 32 GW by 2040. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) has developed a System Planning Test (SPLAT) to model capacity expansion for Africa, illustrating expected changes under a 2019 reference scenario.

South Africa's renewable deployment has already exceeded the model's 2019 baseline, which accounts for the discontinuity at the gap.

The majority of renewable deployment in South Africa is expected to come from solar PV and wind, accounting for an estimated 37% of domestic demand by 2040. IRENA's projections show coal generation dropping to 48% by 2035, while solar and wind rise to 39%. The timely deployment of renewable projects will be key to maintaining stable and reliable supply of electricity.

As coal plants are decommissioned over the next decade, constraints on capacity are likely to affect not only domestic reliability, but also South Africa's role in regional electricity trade.

Regional Electricity Trade

Regional electricity trade provides a mechanism to address the introduced supply gaps and flexibility constraints.

South Africa has acted as a net exporter in the Southern African Power Pool (SAPP) since 2003, supplying electricity to neighbouring countries including Eswatini, Botswana, Zambia, and Lesotho. Regional integration is expected to expand through initiatives such as the African Clean Energy Corridor (ACEC), which links the Southern and Eastern African power pools. IRENA models six scenarios to illustrate a range of possible outcomes of this expansion. These include a reference scenario (base case), variations in renewable deployment, changes in hydrology and degree of regional integration.

The diversity of resources in the ACEC region provides an opportunity for countries to benefit from efficiency gains, with total trade flows projected to grow to five times their 2020 level. The largest estimated increase seen in the case of limited renewable deployment.

A higher degree of renewable deployment results in lower total trade volumes, with the largest consumer countries—Egypt, South Africa, Kenya, Ethiopia—producing more domestic power and therefore importing less.

From IRENA's model[1], in all scenarios, South Africa becomes the largest net importer of electricity in the region by 2040, at 62 TWh net imports in the reference scenario—a notable reversal from SA's role as a net exporter in the SAPP.

The SPLAT model uses data from the EAPP (2014) and SAPP (2017) master plans for assumptions on demand, costs and generation capacity. Projected country trade positions reflect these model assumptions and should be interpreted in this context.

While trade roles are affected by VRE deployment, the entire region benefits from the resulting efficiency gains, seen by the projected increase in total trade flows across each scenario.

Cross-border electricity trade should create resilience rather than dependency in order for all countries to benefit. This requires coordination between countries to ensure projects are delivered on schedule. Interconnected grids must also provide access to transparent data to support future planning and ensure efficiency of trade.
