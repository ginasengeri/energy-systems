South Africa's Energy Situation

Starting in 2007, significant energy shortages led to an electricity crisis which reached its peak in 2022, with 205 days of load shedding. South Africa's load shedding has commonly been attributed to insuffcient maintenance of coal plants and poor governance. However, significant progress has since been made with no load shedding since March 2024. There are several layers that play a role in the future of South Africa's energy landscape:

  1. Coal repurposing
  2. Expansion of renewables
  3. Role reversals in regional trade

Progress in one layer can hide vulnerability in another. Each solution introduces a new constraint which affects the state of an adjacent layer; all interact and must be managed carefully. This makes South Africa a useful example because its electricity system has undergone significant stress and transformation in recent years, and explains how constraints move through the system.

Operational Recovery

Reduced load shedding in South Africa is primarily driven by improved performance of coal power stations as well as the adoption of private renewable energy. Over the financial year 2025-26, Eskom's Energy Availability Factor (EAF) reached or exceeded the 70% milestone on 83 occasions. More than 4100 MW of private capacity was added in the first half of 2025 [3].

Diesel-fired Open Cycle Gas Turbines (OCGTs) were previously used to reduce the severity of load-shedding, and have seen significant decline in usage over 2025. These units are both inefficient (34-36% thermal efficiency) and costly, with diesel generation approximately 11 times more expensive than coal in 2025 (6084R/MWh vs 546R/MWh)[4].

Between April 2025 and March 2026, OCGT generation reduced by 460 GWh, corresponding to an approximate reduction in diesel expenditure of R1.87bn.

This decline reflects both improved coal plant perforamance and reduced system demand due to private generation. Lower reliance on diesel also alleviates pressure on fuel supply for other sectors, particularly transportation.

Coal Repurposing & Renewables Deployment

While short-term reliability has improved, South Africa’s long-term energy strategy introduces a different set of constraints.

South Africa's coal fleet was designed for baseload operation, where output remains above a minimum stable load (MSL). Operating below this threshold can create uneven thermal gradients across boilers, leading to thermal stress and long-term material fatigue. Older plants are especially constrained due to slower responses to thermal instability.

As a result, even at relatively low levels of renewable penetration (just over 10%), this inflexibility has already led to curtailment of low-cost renewable energy, as the system is unable to adjust output quickly enough to accommodate renewables.

Alongside this, long-term plans to reduce coal capacity create the challenge to replace lost baseload capacity on schedule. As part of the Just Energy Transition (JET), Eskom plans to decommission nine coal power plants by 2035 and reduce coal capacity from 39 GW to 18 GW. These sites are being repurposed for renewable energy with a goal of increasing renewable capacity to 32 GW by 2040.

Since 2021, solar and wind capacity has more than doubled from 6% to 12%, driven by private generation investment during the period of heavy load shedding.

As coal plants are decommissioned over the next decade, constraints on capacity are likely to affect not only domestic reliability, but also South Africa’s role in regional electricity trade.

Regional Electricity Trade

South Africa has been a net exporter in the SAPP for the majority of its years in operation since 1995. Eswatini, Botswana, Zambia and Lesotho rely on these exports.

The African Clean Energy Corridor (ACEC) is an initiative supported by The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) which aims to strengthen cooperation between the Southern African Power Pool and the Eastern African Power Pool.

From IRENA's report [1], in all scenarios, South Africa becomes the single largest net importer of electricity in the entire region by 2040, at 62 TWh net imports in the reference scenario. This is a complete structural reversal from SA's role as a net exporter in the SAPP.

The report projects SA's generation mix shifting significantly toward solar PV and wind by 2040. Solar and wind generation in SA alone would be 65–80 GW.

There is a large amount of uncertainty in planning. Future demand, technology costs, fuel prices, political positions and climate impacts across regions are unknown.

What Comes Next?

In addition to cross-border trade, batteries, pumped-hydro etc. coal remains an availble resource that can be utilized responsibly. Coal can be repurposed to improve flexibility addressing curtailment problem.

Redesign to coal plants to improve ability to ramp up and down quickly. Flexible operation reduces the amount of coal burned overall which both saves cost on burning expensive fuel when cheap options are available, and reduces the amount of pollutants released. It also preserves some usable capacity to prevent gaps in total installed capacity while renewables are deployed.

Cross-border trade pays through efficiency gains through lack of need to build more expensive peaking plants or curtail cheap renewable energy.

2021 IRENA report shows the long-term trajectory is SA becoming more dependent on regional imports. Is the region prepared to meet the demand that SA will create?
