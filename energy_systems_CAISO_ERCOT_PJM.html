BESS provide a wide range of services to the grid including energy shifting, frequency regulation and balancing services to buffer variability from renewables. Across the three grids, different levels of penetration highlight the effects of increasing BESS on the grid. Differences and similarities in market designs (energy-only, ERCOT, and capacity market, CAISO) present varying opportunities for the integration of battery storage.

Operational Impact Parameters, Price & Market Signal Parameters

CAISO has the highest BESS penetration followed by ERCOT, with PJM still emerging. The operational impact of batteries is most visible in CAISO, where the duck curve is pronounced and batteries are used for daily load shifting with a mean duration of 3.8 hours. ERCOT experiences more volatile prices due to wind fluctuations, with the 1.56-hour-average batteries used for fast response to price spikes in addition to predictable load shifting. PJM's market structure is similar to CAISO's with a focus on capacity market and reliability, with most queued batteries expected to have a duration 4-hours. PJM's prices currently show volatility similar to ERCOT, suggesting a potential use of batteries for both cyclic load shifting and price arbitrage.

Across all 3 grids, the highest average load is seen in Q3.

More volatile markets need faster response. Batteries can discharge at max rated power almost instantly, providing energy services in ERCOT and PJM grids to meet demand during peaks. This can help lower the price spikes as seen, as BESS expands. CAISO with most penetration has the lowest average price spreads, falling to $2.28 in Q2, and a max of $56.72 in Q3. Unlike ERCOT max $81.63 and PJM max $85.60 both in Q3. Mins remain at $17.52 in Q1 in ERCOT, and $22.12 in Q3 in PJM.

CAISO has longest duration batteries, allowing for steady discharge of energy to meet demand in peak hours of the evening when solar decreases, while allowing other backup generation to come online so filling the gap in the meantime (CCGT requires 1-3hrs to get to full capacity). Batteries don't discharge forever, so backup is needed. The backup e.g. gas needs time from cold-start because during the day, as seen in CAISO, net load falls sometimes to negative values.

After wind and solar (VRE) is produced, net load drops, sometimes below zero. So gas and other thermal sources must be turned off, expensive fuel should not be burnt when cheap renewable energy is available. In the case of negative net load, batteries can consume this extra electricity, charging during the period of low prices and discharging in the evening when prices are higher, earning from this spread (load shifting).

Implications

How is PJM's grid expected to change as battery capacity is added? Given that most queued are 4-hour - how will the grid compare to CAISO's 4-hour cohort (4 hours for day-night energy shifting)? What about given the increasing load - ERCOT has significant battery capacity (1.56 hours, for fast changes e.g. frequency and volatility) but doesn't see the same net load curve due to more volatility (wind) and increasing demand (data centres). PJM needs batteries that serve for reliability and backup services.

Batteries pair well with capacity requirements because they can dispatch at full capacity instantly, guaranteeing supply during peak demand. However they are limited to short intervals (1-4 hours currently) and do not replace need for backup generation. They only fill an immediate gap when demand rises quickly, allowing other technologies the time to ramp up.

CAISO and PJM are more contract-like, with capacity payments to fulfill resource adequacy obligations (FERC rules - more stable revenue, support underwriting in addition to market revenue), while ERCOT is more trading-like, real-time arbitrage, incentives are more immediate price driven, not capacity payments.

Many data centres are building on-site generation and bypassing the grid so in the near term prices are not likely to spike. In ERCOT, the opportunity for batteries is in hedging against worst performing hours (when prices are high, batteries are discharged to ease the price spike).

This works in ERCOT because it has more extreme price swings prices to hedge against. But this doesn't work the same way in CAISO because of resource adequacy requirements (batteries can be partially committed ahead of time, reducing ability to optimize around real-time prices). Also revenues become weaker as batteries saturate the market. Not as extreme spikes, smaller price spread; need a different, more diverse revenue strategy. Driver remains the need for resource adequacy.

For PJM, the biggest driver is the capacity market (ensure enough supply during peak hours, especially with aging thermal plants, increasing demand, strong reliability rules), but needs underwriting. That's FTM. For BTM, transmission savings and credits and capacity value (avoiding peak demand charges) are the main value additions.
