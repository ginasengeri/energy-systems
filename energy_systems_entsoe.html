Electricity Trade in the DE-LU Bidding Zone

This report discusses the roles played by different generation systems, focussing on the Germany-Luxembourg, France, Netherlands and Norway South bidding zones.

ENTSO-E Transparency Platform • 2025
DE-LU · Germany–Luxembourg FR · France NO2 · Norway South NL · Netherlands

System Overview

The European Network Transmission System Operator for Electricity (ENTSO-E) coordinates trade across 36 countries in Europe, ensuring energy security and market efficiency for its member countries[1]. Germany sits between several different power systems in Europe, and connects a diverse set of generation systems. This allows for observation of a range of trade patterns in one place. The focus of this analysis is on electricity transmission between the Germany-Luxembourg bidding zone (DE-LU), France (FR), Netherlands (NL), and Norway South (NO2). This combines a nuclear-heavy system in France, a hydro-dominated system in Norway, and a gas/wind mix in the Netherlands.

France consistently has the largest average price spread against DE-LU except in Q1, where Norway's prices are on average 46 €/MWh cheaper than DE-LU. The Netherlands remains closest to DE-LU in price across all quarters.

There are three main roles observed in the Germany-Luxembourg zone:

  1. Baseload imports — France supplies large, steady volumes of low-cost nuclear power.
  2. Seasonal balancing — Norway's hydro exports vary with reservoir cycles, peaking in winter and falling in summer.
  3. Short-term balancing — the Netherlands provides flexible gas and wind capacity to cover short-term gaps on either side.

Key Insights

  1. The DE-LU zone is a net importer of electricity with 32.8 TWh in imports in 2025, with a majority of imports coming from France (20 TWh).
  2. NO2's average net exports to DE-LU are twice as large in winter as in summer, consistent with seasonal hydro patterns (Q2: 502 MW, Q4: 1030 MW).
  3. DE-LU → NL corridor is the only boundary where the average net flow reverses direction, with net exports out of DE-LU occurring in 10 out of 53 weeks in 2025.

Price differences across zones reflect the composition of each generation fleet. France's nuclear power keeps prices structurally below Germany's, resulting in steady imports into DE-LU. Norway's hydropower has near-zero marginal cost[3] but varies with reservoir levels and precipitation, resulting in declining exports mid-year. The Netherlands relies on gas for 35% of output alongside wind and solar, giving it a price structure closer to Germany-Luxembourg.

Spearman correlation is used to account for non-linear relationships between price spreads and flows. France and Norway show moderate to strong correlations (0.48 and 0.64), indicating stronger responses to price differences. The Netherlands shows a weaker correlation (0.26), suggesting flows are less driven by price incentives. This is consistent with the 10 weeks of net flow reversals between DE-LU and NL, which occur throughout the year with no clear seasonal pattern.

Implications

Prior to 2023, Germany was a net exporter of electricity for two decades. The phasing out of coal and the shutdown of remaining nuclear plants in 2023 has reduced Germany's domestic capacity, contributing to the observabed rise in imports. However these are being offset by the increase of renewable capacity - wind and solar.

LEAG, Germany's second-largest power producer, is phasing out its lignite (brown coal) power plants, with complete shutdown by 2038[5]. Many of LEAG's 8,000 coal workers are expected to retire by 2030 or retrain in renewable energy

Increased generation volatility and price fluctuations have increased the need for battery storage systems in the region. Currently, just under 25 GWh of storage is installed in total, with large-scale systems rising from 2.3 to 3.7 GWh in 2025 alone. It is estimated that 100 to 170 GWh of storage will be needed by 2030[4]. Also investing in hydrogen and battery storage.
