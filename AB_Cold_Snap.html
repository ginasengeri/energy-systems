Alberta's January Price Spike Was Not Caused by Cold

During the first week of January 2026, wind primarily determined when prices spiked, rather than temperature.

Data: AESO

What happened

On January 4th, Alberta recorded temperatures as low as −31°C, 10°C lower than previous days. Prices remained relatively low throughout the drop as wind generation climbed during the day.

The price spike came the following morning, as temperatures began to recover and wind generation fell. At 11am on January 5th, Alberta's pool price reached $858 CAD/MWh — the highest of the month.

```
Date Temperature range Wind at 11am (MW) Pool price at 11am (CAD/MWh)
Jan 4 −31°C to −12°C 261 MW $52.84
Jan 5 −13°C to +2°C 353 MW $858.44
Jan 6 −9°C to +1°C 3,782 MW $0.00

All figures at 11am MPT. Jan 4 had low wind but low price — demand context and time-of-day factors also influence outcomes. Jan 5 and Jan 6 illustrate the wind-price relationship most directly.

```

What this shows

Cold weather increases electricity demand. But in Alberta's current generation mix — where wind supplies roughly 19% of output — wind availability has become a primary driver of price outcomes, sometimes outweighing demand signals. Wind chills increase demand but also increase generation, and the data shows generation outweighing demand spikes in this case.

Volatility of pricing is a structural consequence of integrating variable renewables into an energy-only market. When wind runs, it suppresses prices close to zero. When it slows, gas sets the clearing price which can be very high at periods of high demand.

The broader point

The January 5th event illustrates a pattern where prices reflect wind availability sometimes more than demand conditions. This means that a market designed around dispatchable generation — where demand largely determined price — faces new dynamics that a single uniform price cannot fully capture. This is part of what motivates Alberta's ongoing debate about market redesign.
