Wind Volatility and the Limits of Alberta's Single-Price Market

As renewables grow, uniform electricity pricing struggles to reflect real system conditions. Here is what January 2026 data shows.

Data: AESO

The variability problem

Unlike dispatchable generation, wind produces power on the resource's schedule. In January 2026, wind output varied at four times the rate of gas, which characterizes the nature of this fuel type.

Gas supplies roughly 75% of Alberta's generation and moves the most in megawatt terms every hour, but remains the most stable relative to its size. Wind, at around 19% of generation, fluctuates more relative to its contribution. Gas is used to balance wind fluctuations, with a correlation of −0.65 between the two.

Fig. 1 — Relative variability by fuel type, January 2026

Price suppression

With wind at near-zero marginal cost, high wind output hours tend to suppress pool prices. As wind generation increases, higher-cost gas generators are forced to sell at market prices below their fixed costs.

The same variability that suppresses prices during high wind creates price spikes when output drops. One example is the spike on January 5th at 11am, where pool price reached reached $858 CAD/MWh with wind at 353 MW.

The investment signal problem

Gas generators are still needed for backup when wind drops. However they are only able to recover their fixed costs in high-price hours. In a market where prices are suppressed or spike unpredictably, it becomes difficult to justify building the capacity the system actually needs.

A uniform province-wide price cannot distinguish between cheap power that is genuinely useful and cheap power that is overwhelming the system. As renewables grow, this needs to be addresed in market design.

Why this points to market redesign

Alberta is actively debating a transition to Locational Marginal Pricing — a fundamental redesign of how electricity is priced across the grid. LMP would more accurately reflect real-time scarcity and system conditions, giving both generators and investors clearer signals about when and where capacity is actually needed. The data above does not prove LMP is the answer. It does show that the structural dynamics motivating the debate are already observable in Alberta's system today.
