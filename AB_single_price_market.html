Wind Volatility and the Limits of Alberta's Single-Price Market

January 2026 data shows how renewable variability shapes price behavior in Alberta's electricity market.

AESO • January 2026

Alberta operates in an energy-only electricity market where generators receive a single uniform price for the power they produce. As renewable generation grows, this pricing structure struggles to reflect local congestion and scarcity conditions. January 2026 data illustrates these dynamics in Alberta's electricity system.

Key Market Insights

  1. Wind generation was four times more variable than gas in January 2026.
  2. Gas generation balances wind variability (−0.65 correlation).
  3. High wind output suppresses prices, while low wind during high demand can trigger price spikes.
  4. These dynamics weaken investment signals in Alberta's energy-only market.

Three Days in January

Wind, demand, and weather conditions all influence pool prices. Despite the cold and low wind generation on January 4th, prices remained relatively low due to lower weekend demand. On January 5th at 11 a.m., the pool price reached 858 CAD/MWh with the return of weekday demand and wind generation at 353 MW. By January 6th prices fell to 0 CAD/MWh as wind generation increased significantly.

Similar price spikes have occurred during other periods of demand stress. During the January 2024 cold snap, for example, wind generation was low and pool prices reached the market cap of 999.99 CAD/MWh several times.

The Variability Problem

Unlike dispatchable generation, wind produces power on the resource's schedule. In January 2026, gas supplied roughly 75% of Alberta's total output and was the most stable source relative to its average output. Wind, at around 19%, fluctuated much more relative to its contribution.

Fig. 1 — Relative variability by fuel type (coefficient of variation), January 2026

Variability is measured as the standard deviation of hourly output divided by mean generation (coefficient of variation). Solar and hydro are included for completeness; the primary comparison is between wind and gas, which make up over 94% of Alberta's generation mix.

Gas variability is largely in response to wind fluctuations, with operators ramping generation up and down to maintain system balance. This dynamic is reflected in the −0.65 correlation between the two.

The Investment Signal Problem

With wind at near-zero marginal cost, high wind output hours tend to suppress pool prices. As wind generation increases, higher-cost gas generators are dispatched for fewer hours, reducing their revenues and weakening investment signals.

Alberta recorded 91 hours with pool prices at 0 CAD/MWh in January 2026.

Gas generators provide essential security when wind drops. In a market where prices are suppressed or spike unpredictably and revenues are uncertain, it becomes difficult to justify investment in the capacity the system actually needs.

A uniform province-wide price does not always distinguish between cheap power that is useful and cheap power that is overwhelming the system. For example, surplus generation in one part of the grid may suppress prices even when transmission constraints prevent that electricity from reaching higher-demand areas.

This lack of resolution results in dispatch inefficiency, and weakens price signals for where new generation is most valuable. As renewables grow, more precise pricing is required to reflect conditions across the system.

Restructuring Alberta's Energy-Only Market

In 2016, Alberta began transitioning from an energy-only to a capacity market, where generators are paid to be available regardless of whether they are running or not. This transition was cancelled in 2019, with the government returning to the energy-only model to maintain affordability and a simpler market structure. Since then, increased Energy Emergency Alerts (EEAs) and high-price events have led to the need for restructuring.

The Alberta Electric System Operator (AESO) is implementing the Restructured Energy Market (REM), which includes the introduction of Locational Marginal Pricing (LMP), an increased market cap to 1500 CAD/MWh (2027) and 2000 CAD/MWh (2032), and a 30-minute ramping reserve product (R30).

Under LMP, electricity prices vary by location based on local supply, demand, and transmission constraints. This allows prices to reflect congestion and scarcity conditions more accurately and provides clearer signals about where generation and flexibility are most needed.

In addition, the price floor will drop from 0 CAD/MWh to -100 CAD/MWh in 2032 to incentivize reduced generation during periods of oversupply. These adjustments aim to improve efficiency and reliability, while maintaining investor support and keeping electricity affordable.
